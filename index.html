175 markets

Australia has no official register of farmers' markets. Scraping the only national directory turns up 175 markets which are concentrated so unevenly that half the country's local government areas are more than an hour's drive from one.

This is what that directory looks like when you put it on a map, and what it says about who farmers' markets actually serve.

Section 1 — Where can you find a market?

A south-eastern, small-town institution

The Australian Farmers' Markets Association directory is the de facto national census. It holds 178 listings, which resolve to 175 distinct markets once duplicates are removed. New South Wales and Victoria hold 70% of them between them. Queensland, despite having a much larger horticultural output, has only thirteen.

The “grassroots” framing needs qualifying. Most markets are run by a local committee, church, Rotary club or school P&C — but the busiest metropolitan ones cluster under a few professional operators, and those operators are what a shopper in Melbourne actually encounters.

Section 2 — How far to your nearest market?

Markets are dense where people are thin

Per head of population the pattern inverts what you would expect. Rest-of-Victoria and rest-of-NSW have the highest market density in the country; Greater Brisbane, with 2.8 million people, has two. Regional towns are well served relative to their size. The problem is that there are not many of them, and they are far apart.

Only 47% of Australia's local government areas are within 50 km of a farmers' market, covering 5% of the land mass — and 86% of markets trade on a weekend morning. If you work Saturday mornings, most of the national network is closed to you. Whatever farmers' markets do for sustainable shopping, they do it for a particular kind of shopper, in a particular kind of place, at a particular time of week.

Section 3 — How are markets good for producers?

A good channel, not a livelihood

The best evidence on stallholders is a 2014 RIRDC survey of 286 of them — dated, but still the richest Australian dataset that exists. It shows a genuinely local supply base: 57% of stallholders travel 50 km or less to their main market, and the producer keeps the sale price minus a stall fee that is typically under $80.

This is the honest version of “markets support local producers”. They are a high-margin, customer-facing channel that most stallholders use alongside local shops, restaurants and wholesale — not a replacement for them. That is a real benefit, and it is a smaller claim than the one usually made.

Section 4 — Are markets better for the environment?

Food miles are the weakest argument available

The intuitive case for farmers' markets is transport emissions. The evidence does not support it. Across almost every food, transport is a thin slice of the total footprint — usually under 10%, often under 5%. What you buy matters far more than how far it came.

There is a stronger argument, and it is about waste rather than distance. Almost a third of Australia's food waste happens before food leaves the farm — a large share of it rejected on cosmetic specifications set by retail buyers. Farmers' markets have no cosmetic spec.

A market stall that sells the misshapen carrot is doing more for emissions than the short drive it arrived on. And since 27% of stallholders sell fruit and vegetables — foods roughly two orders of magnitude below beef per kilogram — the composition of what markets sell matters more than their supply chain. Both arguments hold. Neither is the food-miles one.

Section 5 — Markets and Supermarkets

Two chains, two-thirds of the groceries

The scale comparison is stark. Coles and Woolworths together take about 67% of Australian supermarket grocery revenue. At a farmers' market the producer keeps the sale price less a stall fee, with no wholesaler, distributor or retail margin in between — a structurally different arrangement, on a vastly different scale.

175 markets, most of them monthly, most of them open for four hours on a Saturday morning. Farmers' markets are not an alternative food system at national scale, and reading them as one sets them up to fail. What they are is a direct channel that works well for the producers who can reach one and the shoppers who live near one — and the map is the argument for widening both.

Sources and caveats

  • There is no official dataset of Australian farmers' markets. No ABS series, no data.gov.au entry. The AFMA directory is self-registration, so it under-counts small and non-member markets — 175 is a floor, not a census.
  • The stallholder data is twelve years old. RIRDC 14/040 (2014) surveyed 286 stallholders and 61 market managers. Nothing more recent or more comprehensive exists. Treat it as indicative.
  • Distances are as the crow flies, from LGA area centroids rather than population-weighted centres, so remote LGAs with a single coastal town read as further from a market than their residents actually are.
  • Trading frequency and day are derived from a free-text field. Four listings disagree with their own opening-hours table; those and every other cleaning decision are logged in data/data_quality.csv.
  • Vintages differ. AFMA 2026 · ABS population 30 June 2025 · ABS boundaries 2021 (LGA 2023) · ACCC 2025 · DCCEEW 2016-17 · Poore & Nemecek 2018 · RIRDC 2014.
