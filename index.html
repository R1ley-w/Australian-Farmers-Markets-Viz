Australian Farmer's Markets

This project aims to visualise various metrics on Farmer's Markets in Australia, it use data from the national Farmer's Market directory which contains 175 markets which are concentrated mostly in eastern Australia and are generally not easy to get to for most Australians.

Section 1 Where can you find a market?

The Australian Farmers' Markets Association directory was the main source of data I used. It holds 178 listings, which resolve to 175 markets (after removing duplicates). New South Wales and Victoria hold 70% of them, Queensland, despite having a much larger crop output, only has thirteen.

Most markets are run by a local committee (including my local market), church, Rotary club or school, but the busiest metropolitan ones cluster under a few more professional organisers.

Section 2 How far to the nearest market?

Markets tend to crop up in rural areas far more frequently than highly populated ones. Rural Victoria and Rural NSW have the highest market density in the country; Greater Brisbane, with 2.8 million people, only has two. Regional towns are well served relative to their size. The problem is that there are not many of them, and they are far apart.

I'm fortunate enough to be within the 47% of Australia's local government areas are within 50 km of a farmers' market. These 'accessible areas' cover only 5% of the land mass, furthermore 86% of markets trade on a weekend morning. If you work Saturday mornings, most of the national network is closed to you. Whatever farmers' markets do for sustainable shopping, they do it for a particular kind of shopper, in a particular kind of place, at a particular time of week.

Section 3 Where is our food produced?

Australian farms produced $70.9 billion of food and fibre in 2020-21. Meat and grain make up almost two thirds of it. Fruit, nuts and vegetables, the staples of a market stall, are under a fifth.

Each tile is a commodity, sized by the gross value farms produced. Click a tile to see where it is grown on the map below, and click it again to clear.

On the map, each council gets one symbol: its shape is the main type of produce, its size the value per hectare. Click a state to zoom in. Queensland's Lockyer Valley, Bundaberg, Whitsunday and Burdekin grow the most vegetables in the country, yet the state has only 13 markets.

The intuitive case for farmers' markets is transport emissions. The evidence does not support it. Across almost every food, transport is a thin slice of the total footprint — usually under 10%, often under 5%. What you buy matters far more than how far it came.

There is a stronger argument, and it is about waste rather than distance. Almost a third of Australia's food waste happens before food leaves the farm — a large share of it rejected on cosmetic specifications set by retail buyers. Farmers' markets have no cosmetic spec.

A market stall that sells the misshapen carrot is doing more for emissions than the short drive it arrived on. 57% of stallholders travel 50 km or less, but transport is a thin slice of any food's footprint. What a market sells, and what it saves from waste, matters more than how far it came.

Section 4 Can markets compete with the big two?

At a farmers' market the producer keeps the sale price minus a stall fee, which is typically under $80. There is no wholesaler, distributor or retail margin in between. The best evidence on stallholders is a 2014 RIRDC survey of 286 of them: dated, but still the richest Australian dataset that exists. The competition is Coles and Woolworths.

The scale comparison is stark. Coles and Woolworths together take about 67% of Australian supermarket grocery revenue — a structurally different arrangement, on a vastly different scale.

This is the honest version of “markets support local producers”. They are a high-margin, customer-facing channel that most stallholders use alongside local shops, restaurants and wholesale — not a replacement for them, and not an alternative food system at national scale. What they are is a direct channel that works well for the producers who can reach one and the shoppers who live near one.
